How often should commercial windows be cleaned?

How businesses can set sensible commercial window cleaning intervals by considering footfall, weather, access, presentation and safety.

23 November 2026

All articles

There is no single cleaning frequency that works for every commercial property. A busy shopfront beside a main road will collect fingerprints and traffic film differently from first-floor office glazing in a sheltered business park. The useful question is how quickly the windows become noticeably dirty and what level of presentation the premises needs.

Start with the type of building and its visitors

Ground-floor retail glass usually needs closer attention because customers see it at eye level and touch it frequently. Restaurants, cafes and hospitality venues may also need regular entrance and front-window cleaning where presentation matters every day.

Offices and managed commercial buildings can have different priorities. Some reception areas need frequent cleaning while upper-storey windows are better handled on a separate planned cycle. High-level glazing may be accessed less often because the work requires different equipment and safe arrangements.

Use a trial interval rather than a fixed national rule

For planning, some businesses compare weekly or fortnightly shopfront visits with monthly or less frequent visits to sheltered offices. These are examples of schedules to assess, not a recommended frequency or market standard. The right interval depends on the actual premises.

Review the glass a few days before the next planned clean. If the entrance already looks poor, the schedule may be too far apart. If the windows remain presentable, there may be room to adjust the frequency. Take account of seasonal changes rather than assuming the whole year behaves the same.

Consider roads, trees and local conditions

Traffic spray can leave a film on street-facing glass. Trees may deposit pollen, sap or leaf debris. Exposed properties experience different conditions from sheltered courtyards. Nearby building work can increase dust, while winter weather may leave more marks around frames and lower windows.

If one elevation gets dirty much more quickly than another, discuss cleaning those areas more often instead of paying for identical attention to every part of the building on each visit.

Separate exterior from interior requirements

Internal glass, entrance doors, partitions and display windows can collect fingerprints even when the outside glazing looks fine. A commercial quotation should state clearly whether it covers external windows, internal windows, frames, sills, glass doors, rooflights and any specialist glazing.

Set separate intervals where appropriate. The person managing the premises should know which surfaces will be cleaned on each visit and who is responsible for opening rooms or arranging access.

Plan safe access and disruption

High or hard-to-reach windows need an appropriate method and risk assessment. Do not arrange unsafe ladder work merely to maintain an arbitrary timetable. The Health and Safety Executive's guidance on working at height explains the general requirement to plan and manage such work safely.

For customer-facing premises, agree when work can be carried out without blocking entrances or disrupting deliveries. If a building is managed, make sure contractors know the access procedure and who should receive reports or raise defects.

What should a recurring commercial quote include?

Ask for a schedule identifying the elevations, glass types, visit frequency, included surfaces, access requirements and any periodic high-level work. Clarify how missed visits, unsuitable weather, price reviews and additional cleaning requests will be handled.

A cleaner should be able to explain what they can inspect from the ground and when specialist access is needed. Window cleaning does not replace repairs to damaged glazing, frames or seals.

Review the plan as the property changes

A new tenant, different shop opening hours or a change in landscaping can affect what the building needs. Review whether the existing schedule is still serving staff, customers and managers rather than automatically renewing an unsuitable arrangement.
